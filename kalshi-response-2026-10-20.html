RE: Kalshi's "Who Calibrates Markets"¶

What must a taker strategy prove before it trades real money, and how close are we?

Short summary. Kalshi's research team published "Who Calibrates Markets? Evidence from Trader Micro-Data" (Kagan and Baiocchi, October 2026) on 19 Oct. Their headline is that about 0.15 % of Kalshi accounts forecast better than the price they trade against, after correcting for luck, and that the price moves their way after they trade. We build racinglines (web demo, repo), a model that prices motorsport markets (Formula 1, NASCAR, MotoGP and more) and strategies that trade on it. So we turned their paper into five rules a taker strategy must pass before it trades real money, and then ran every historical test our data allows to see where we stand.

The short answer: our model is a useful second opinion in F1 2026 and not yet more than that. - Scored on every F1 market it priced this season, traded or not, the model is as accurate as the market overall. Where it disagrees with the price by more than 5¢, it was right more often than wrong: about half of its predicted edge showed up in the results, on both Kalshi and Polymarket. - Every strategy setting we have saved, 20 on Kalshi and 106 on Polymarket, bought on the right side of the market's mid in 2026 backtests. None passes the luck test once every setting tried is counted, and none holds in both 2025 and 2026. In 2025, and in NASCAR, the model loses to the market. - After our Kalshi trades, the price kept moving our way until the race. That is the paper's mark of an informed trader, but it is measured on backtest trades, not real ones. - So rule 1 (skill, not luck) is partly met for F1 2026 only, and the deciding tests are now forward: our locked per-race strategies and a pre-registered Polymarket holdout, both from the US Grand Prix in Austin (23 to 25 Oct).

Why this paper matters to us

A prediction market price is a crowd's forecast. The paper asks who makes that forecast accurate, and finds a small group of traders who buy and sell against the price and are right more often than it is. Racinglines is an attempt to be one of those traders, in motorsport, with a bottoms-up model and a lot of historical race data. Our model simulates each race from the drivers' recent results, practice and qualifying, and turns that into a fair price for every market it can: who wins, who makes the podium, head-to-heads and more. A strategy then decides when a gap between our fair price and the market's is worth trading.

The paper gives us a way to check ourselves that most backtests skip. A backtest can look profitable because one of many settings got lucky, because it fills at prices nobody could have traded at, or because the season it was tuned on was unusual. The paper's tests (skill against the mid, a luck test over every account, what happens to the price after a trade) answer each of those. They are also the right tests for anyone, a customer included, who wants to trade on a model.

The answer: five golden rules

Before any taker strategy is deployed with real money on Kalshi or any prediction market, by us or by a customer, it must prove all five. A customer proves them on their own fees, latency and size. We adopted these as our own rules on 19 Oct, and every taker strategy we build is now checked against them. They open our development guide (#274) and the trading chapter of our internal book (3.4 Trading).

2. Out of sample. It holds forward on pre-registered locks, not on the season it was tuned on. - Paper: sec 4.4, Table 4 - Where we are: per-event strategy locks, committed before each race and scored as bet (since 15 Oct, #213), and a pre-registered Polymarket test (#276) whose blind half starts at Austin.

3. The real book. 4. The price comes our way. After entry the mid moves toward our side and doesn't reverse. - Paper: sec 3.4, 5.4, Fig 9 - Where we are: markouts of every backtest trade, at the next stage of the race weekend and at the last one before the race, plus closing-line value (#223).

5. Paper reconciles with real. Simulated fills match real fills before any money goes in. - Paper: (ours, not the paper's) - Where we are: our go/no-go goal is CLV above +2 % over 100 live bets and six live race weekends that reconcile. No real money yet.

3. Where racinglines stands: every historical test we can run (20 Oct)

Two questions run through this section. First, is the model's price better than the market's? That is a question about the model alone, so we score it on every market it prices. Second, is a gap between our price and the market's an edge worth trading? That is a question about strategies, so we score every strategy setting we have ever saved, with the fills a real trader would have got. The paper answers both for Kalshi's traders; we answer them for ourselves.

Everything here is a backtest on a mirror of our production database, read-only. Prices and fills come from two tapes: our own recorder's ("our tape") and Probalytics' full order books, which carry every level and size ("Probalytics' books"). All intervals are 95 % and resample whole race weekends, because outcomes within one race are linked.

3.1 The model on every market it prices

What this tests. Here the model is the strategy: no tuned thresholds, no selection. At every stage of every race weekend (before the weekend, after each practice, after qualifying), we take every market the model prices and the venue's price at that moment, whether or not we traded. Two numbers come out: - Skill score against the market (the paper's BSS): above zero means the model's probabilities beat the market's on the outcomes. - The edge curve. For each market, the predicted edge is our price minus the market's, and the realized edge is the outcome minus the market's price. If the slope of realized on predicted is 1, the model's edge is all real; if it is 0, none of it is. This is what decides "a fair price" vs "an edge worth trading": a strategy should trade only where the part of the edge that is real clears the costs.

What it says: - F1 2026: about half the model's edge is real. The slope is close to 0.5 on both venues and both tapes, with three of the four intervals above zero. Overall the model is no better than the market (skill ≈ 0), but where they disagree by more than 5¢ the model was right more often than wrong. That answers the fair-price question: our price is a second opinion worth about half its face value, so a trade needs a predicted edge of roughly twice its cost. - F1 2025 and NASCAR: no edge. The slope is about zero, and the model loses to the market on accuracy. The worst case is the 2025 Polymarket constructor markets (skill −1.6 over 16 weekends). - The 2025 Polymarket row is our "ready at launch" case. It asks what today's model would have done from F1's first Polymarket markets: it would have lost to them. Our tape starts with those markets, and the answer doesn't need a decay curve, because there was no edge to decay. - Calibrating doesn't change it. The same runs with the model's win prices recalibrated on earlier races only (calib_power = walk_forward) move every number by a few hundredths at most (F1 Kalshi 2026 slope 0.48, F1 Polymarket 2025 skill −0.14, NASCAR 2026 skill −0.10). Calibrating per sport and per market kind, rather than one power on win prices, isn't built yet.

3.3 Every setting tried: the luck test

What this tests. A strategy setting is our choice of thresholds, sizing and timing on top of the model. We have tried many over the past months, and the paper's rule is that a setting's result only counts after correcting for every setting tried. So we re-ran every setting we ever saved: 20 on Kalshi (F1 2025 and 2026, our tape) and 106 on Polymarket (F1, at the touch on Probalytics' books, on the markets that pass the test floor in 3.4). Each trade is scored against the venue's mid, and the false-discovery correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) covers every setting, season and taker mode together.

What it says: - In 2026, every saved setting bought on the right side of the mid, on both venues, and every Polymarket setting stayed positive after costs. That is a property of the model, not of one lucky setting: selection alone would not make all 126 positive. - None passes the full test. On Polymarket no setting survives the correction across all 749 cells (the smallest q is 0.054), and none has a skill score interval above zero. On Kalshi 19 of 20 pass the correction in 2026, but those q-values treat trades as independent, so they're optimistic; the weekend-resampled intervals are the ones to trust, and they clear zero for 11 of 20. - Nothing holds across seasons. No setting has an interval above zero in both 2025 and 2026 on either venue, and the ranking reverses: across settings, 2025's edge and 2026's are negatively correlated (Kalshi −0.51, Polymarket −0.33). Picking a setting by last season's result would have hurt. - So rule 1 is partly met: the 2026 edge is real in the sense that it doesn't depend on the setting, but it is 2026-specific until a forward test agrees. A 2026-only effect, such as a pricing bias in this season's newer F1 markets, fits the data as well as skill does.

3.4 A pre-registered Polymarket test

3.6 The crowd: is motorsport priced fairly?

Over 3.19 million resolved fills on the F1 markets we link (both venues, every month in our Probalytics pull), the price paid tracks the settled frequency at the ends: a Kalshi YES bought at 5¢ settled 6.7 % of the time, and at 95¢, 97.3 %. The middle (35 to 65¢) is noisy because a few big markets dominate it. A per-market weighting is still to do, so we draw no conclusion on the paper's Fig 6 yet.

Against bigger sports. The paper doesn't split Sports by league, so we can't set motorsport against the NFL or NHL directly, and we hold no tape for those leagues. What we can compare is motorsport against the paper's averages:

Our reading, inferred rather than shown: motorsport on Kalshi trades like the paper's Sports markets, late and mostly during the event, but costs about twice as much to take, and it still leaves room for a model in some seasons. That fits a smaller, thinner market than the big leagues that dominate the paper's Sports volume. Polymarket's F1 markets are different again: most of their volume comes long before the race. A direct comparison needs Kalshi's per-sport numbers or our own tape on an NFL or NHL market, and neither is in this report.

The fill sits between the mid and our price, so it keeps the direction the model got right and drops the part of the size that was too big, which fits the edge curve in 3.1 (about half of the predicted edge is real). This reading is inferred. The Kalshi setting goes the other way: +0.05 on the model's price (3.2) and +0.033 on the fill. - Against the first cut: the NASCAR paper bets passed their luck test (p < 0.001), but on a synthetic fill and with no correction for the settings behind them; section 3.2 shows NASCAR's model losing to Kalshi's price once it trades on the real book. The 20 Oct runs replace that pass with real fills, more settings and a stricter test, and nothing passes.

5. Caveats

  1. Simulated, not executed. A mid move after a backtest trade is the market coming round to our view, not our impact. Our own impact on thin books is unmeasured.
  2. The fill model changes the score itself (sec 2.3). We report touch and book-walk fills side by side.
  3. The benchmark mid is estimated wherever we have no recorded book. 2025 Kalshi is estimate-only.
  4. Horizons. Our books are 5-minute snapshots, and we trade at stage cutoffs, while the paper's Sports volume sits in the last tenth of a market's life (Fig 7).
  5. Selection. The false-discovery correction runs over every combination tried. In-sample profiles are never evidence.
  6. Joint outcomes. Our luck test draws each driver independently and ignores "one winner per race", so its p-values are approximate.
  7. Sample size. NASCAR has 40 races and MotoGP 11; the paper's bar is 100 events.
  8. Tape gaps. Our own Kalshi trade tape has a gap from 29 Sep to 9 Oct.
  9. Fees. The skill score ignores fees; profit and CLV include them. We report all three together.
  10. Scope. We have no linked cycling markets on either venue, so cycling, which we model, is not tested here.
  11. Not blind. Every backtest window here, holdouts included, was seen in some form while the model was built. Only the forward tests (the per-event locks and the live Polymarket holdout from Austin) are blind.

7. What we take from this

Kalshi's paper says a market is made accurate by a small group of traders who are right against the price, can be told apart from the lucky by a strict test, and leave a mark: the price moves their way and stays. We held racinglines to the same three tests, on every market our model prices and every strategy setting we have saved. Three things came out.

Our model is worth about half of what it says, in F1 2026. Where it disagrees with the market, about half of the gap shows up in the results, on both venues and both tapes (3.1). That is enough to trade on when the gap is about twice the cost, and not otherwise. It turns the question of "a fair price or an edge worth trading" into one measured number instead of a tuned threshold.

The question is the season, not the setting. Every setting we have saved bought on the right side of the mid in 2026, and at real fills the Polymarket strategies score close to the paper's median skilled trader (4.1). The same settings found nothing in 2025, and the model loses to Kalshi's price in NASCAR. So the luck test that matters is no longer "which setting" but "was 2026 a pattern or a one-off", and no backtest can answer it.

Motorsport is a thin market, and it has room in it. Taking a price costs about twice what it does in the paper's average market (3.6, 4.4), and Kalshi's volume arrives late, like the paper's Sports. After our backtest trades on Kalshi, the price kept moving our way until the race (3.5): the shape the paper finds for its skilled traders, though we haven't traded yet, so our own impact is unknown.

So rule 1 is partly met, rule 3 holds for F1, rule 4 holds on Kalshi in backtests, and rules 2 and 5 are where we start. From hand-off to this page took under 13 hours, most of it unattended runs on our own server (timeline); the slow part from here is the races.

What's next

  • Forward, from Austin (23 to 25 Oct). Each race's locked strategy scores as bet, and the frozen Polymarket pick scores live. This is the only blind test, and rule 2's record starts here.
  • The trade rule from 3.1. Trade only where half the predicted edge clears the spread and fees. It was found on 2026 data, so it is pre-registered before it counts.
  • Finish rule 4. Intervals and de-duplication for our markouts, and the crowd's price impact by order size (paper sec 5.4), which needs Probalytics' day books.

If the 2026 edge is real, Austin is where it starts to show. If it isn't, the locks will say that too, because they were written down first.
